## Headline finding

The naive evaluation (`python -m src.leakage_demo`) gives R² = 1.000 and is invalid, because `casual + registered == cnt` on every row. Under a temporal split (train 2011, validate H1 2012, test H2 2012), the mean baseline scores -2.14 on test because ridership grew about 65%, persistence ("yesterday again") scores 0.51, ridge reaches 0.58, and gradient boosting fails at -0.47 because a tree cannot predict above its training maximum. Ridge beats persistence on test R² but loses on MAE (945.5 vs 840.1 rides), so the honest size of the result is "0.07 R² better than doing nothing clever." Adding `cnt_lag_2` changed validation R² by -0.002 for ridge and +0.030 for hgb; I kept it as harmless but marginal.

## Exercise 4: target transformation

Gradient boosting fails on test (R² -0.47) because a tree predicts by averaging training targets, so it cannot predict above the 2011 maximum, and 2012 ridership grew about 65%. I changed the target from the daily count to the day-to-day change, `cnt - cnt_lag_1`, and added `cnt_lag_1` back to the predictions (`scripts/ex4_target_transform.py`). The change stays in a similar range in both years, so the trees no longer have to extrapolate. On validation, hgb rose from 0.248 to 0.606, while ridge was unchanged (0.645 to 0.646), since a linear model can already use yesterday's count directly. I decided the transformation is defensible before looking at test: `cnt_lag_1` is known at prediction time, so nothing leaks, and the change fixes the specific mechanism behind the failure rather than tuning until a number improves. It also does not simply win the comparison, as ridge still leads. I then touched test once: hgb went from -0.474 to 0.566 R² (MAE 1948 to 896 rides), close to ridge's 0.586. Persistence still has the lowest test MAE (840). The committed `reports/metrics.json` is the untransformed pipeline.

## Exercise 5: ridge vs persistence

Ridge is worth deploying over persistence, but by a narrow and specific margin. On validation it wins on both metrics (R² 0.645 vs 0.451; MAE 833 vs 901 rides). On test it keeps the R² lead (0.583 vs 0.510) but loses on MAE (946 vs 840 rides). The coefficients explain the split: alongside lagged demand (`cnt_roll_7` +458, `cnt_lag_1` +348 per standard deviation), ridge puts substantial weight on weather (`weather_severity` -282, `temp` +185, `windspeed` -158, `hum` -119), information persistence cannot use. So ridge anticipates the large weather-driven swings that dominate squared error, while persistence is closer on ordinary days that dominate absolute error. For an operator rebalancing bikes, missing a storm-day collapse is costlier than being 100 rides off on a typical day, so ridge is the better choice; if the cost were proportional to the daily error, persistence would win, and the 0.07 R² gain would not justify the extra complexity.
